I. ABSTRACT

Project L.O.O.N.A., which stands for Locomotive Optical Operative for Navigation and Assistance, is an advanced, distributed multi-microcontroller and artificial intelligence robotic companion designed to unify autonomous optical vision tracking, natural spoken language interaction, multimodal conversational reasoning, high-current locomotive actuation, and modular hardware expansion. Unlike traditional monolithic robotic prototypes that suffer from processor starvation, bus contention, and memory leaks during continuous operation, LOONA is engineered with a decoupled, four-tier distributed architecture spanning two dedicated 32-bit dual-core ESP32-S3 microcontrollers, an asynchronous edge server brain, and an interactive client web application.

The primary physical chassis is structured as a compact, high-torque four-wheel-drive mobile rover equipped with four independent BTS7960 high-power MOS H-bridge motor drivers driven by an eight-channel, twenty-kilohertz ultrasonic pulse-width modulation matrix that completely eliminates audible motor whine while preventing electrical back-EMF resets. Physical expression is delivered through an operative five-axis servo matrix governing head pan, head tilt, left ear perk, right ear perk, and tail wagging. To maintain high agility and lightweight locomotion, the multi-axis robotic arm has been removed from the primary mobile chassis and is re-engineered as an optional plug-and-play modular add-on attachment that interfaces through dedicated dynamic expansion general-purpose input/output headers without burdening baseline mobility.

Vision and digital audio are processed in real time through dedicated hardware peripherals. An ESP32-S3 camera module captures motion JPEG video at thirty frames per second in quarter video graphics array resolution for closed-loop proportional visual gaze and distance tracking. Digital sound is handled via two dedicated inter-IC sound channels comprising an omnidirectional INMP441 micro-electro-mechanical microphone sampled at sixteen kilohertz for speech recognition and a MAX98357A class-D amplifier delivering synthesized twenty-four kilohertz pulse-code modulation speech to an onboard enclosed speaker. High-level multimodal reasoning is coordinated by Google Gemini artificial intelligence and Edge-TTS voice synthesis running on an asynchronous FastAPI server. To prevent random-access memory degradation during extended multi-turn conversations, all persistent facial embeddings, conversation logs, hardware macro definitions, and photographic captures are indexed inside an embedded SQLite3 relational database vault. The chassis microcontroller embeds its own standalone hypertext transfer protocol web controller on port eighty alongside a high-speed direct user datagram protocol receiver on port eighty-eight-eighty-eight, offering direct mobile browser driving, automatic soft access point failover, and sub-millisecond response times. Experimental testing demonstrates zero packet loss, stable multi-hour operation, and an immersive zero-interface full-screen companion mode with continuous background speech recognition.

Keywords: Mobile Robot, ESP32-S3, BTS7960 H-Bridge, Ultrasonic PWM, I2S Digital Audio, Computer Vision, Multimodal AI, Modular Add-on Attachment, Distributed Embedded Systems.


II. THE PROBLEM AND ITS BACKGROUND

1.1 Background of the Project
Mobile robotic platforms represent a significant technological bridge between mechanical engineering, embedded electronics, and modern artificial intelligence. While traditional automated guided vehicles and mobile robots are typically designed for single-purpose industrial transport, modern companion and operative robots are expected to deliver rich human-robot interaction through synchronized multi-sensory channels. A truly effective operative assistant must see and track its user through visual perception, hear spoken commands through digital acoustic sensors, respond naturally through expressive synthesized speech, navigate its physical environment smoothly, and maintain an engaging physical and virtual persona.

However, implementing these sophisticated capabilities within a self-contained embedded robot introduces severe engineering challenges. Running high-resolution computer vision algorithms, deep learning feature extraction, and neural voice synthesis concurrently demands substantial memory and processing power that easily overwhelm typical standalone microcontrollers. Conversely, relying entirely on a remote desktop host via tethered cabling introduces latency, physical entanglement, and the risk of catastrophic locomotive runaway if communication drops. Project L.O.O.N.A. resolves this dilemma through a distributed cyber-physical architecture. By allocating digital signal acquisition and ultrasonic motor driving to dual ESP32-S3 microcontrollers while delegating multimodal artificial intelligence and computer vision to an asynchronous server brain, LOONA achieves real-time responsiveness, quiet operation, and robust long-term reliability.

1.2 Statement of the Problem
The central problem of this engineering project is: How can multiple embedded microcontrollers, high-current motor drivers, digital audio peripherals, computer vision models, and conversational artificial intelligence be integrated into a stable, low-latency, and safe mobile robot?

Specifically, the study addresses and answers the following technical questions:
First, how can real-time camera video, digital microphone input, and speaker audio be streamed simultaneously across wireless networks without bandwidth congestion, audio stuttering, or buffer under-runs?
Second, how can high-current four-wheel-drive direct-current motors be driven smoothly without generating audible ultrasonic motor whine, inductive electrical interference, or microcontroller brownout resets?
Third, how can optical computer vision tracking guide physical locomotive movement in real time with minimal latency and zero mechanical hunting?
Fourth, how can the robotic system overcome the progressive random-access memory degradation that typically causes large conversational artificial intelligence software to crash during prolonged conversational sessions?
Fifth, how can the robot provide reliable standalone web control and support modular add-on attachments, such as a multi-axis robotic arm, without overloading the physical chassis or compromising baseline mobility?

1.3 Objectives of the Project
The general objective of this project is to design, fabricate, program, and empirically verify Project L.O.O.N.A., a distributed mobile companion robot that integrates optical target tracking, natural voice interaction, multimodal artificial intelligence reasoning, high-current four-wheel-drive locomotion, five-axis servo actuation, modular add-on attachment interfaces, and an embedded web controller.

The specific technical objectives are:
To design and construct Module One, the Head Controller, using an ESP32-S3 camera board to stream thirty-frame-per-second motion JPEG video, capture sixteen-kilohertz digital speech through an INMP441 micro-electro-mechanical microphone over inter-IC sound port zero, and deliver twenty-four-kilohertz synthesized speech through a MAX98357A amplifier over inter-IC sound port one.
To design and construct Module Two, the Chassis Motion Controller, using an ESP32-S3 DevKitC with an eight-channel, twenty-kilohertz ultrasonic hardware pulse-width modulation matrix driving four BTS7960 high-power H-bridges in differential tank-drive configuration and an operative five-axis servo matrix.
To implement a standalone embedded web controller and high-speed user datagram protocol receiver on port eighty-eight-eighty-eight within Module Two, enabling direct smartphone driving, hardware telemetry monitoring, and automatic soft access point hotspot fallback.
To develop Module Three, the Python Brain, featuring an OpenCV proportional closed-loop face-tracking algorithm, Google Gemini multimodal vision reasoning with automated tool calling, dual-channel speech synthesis, an autonomous idle roaming controller with obstacle and person avoidance, and a persistent SQLite3 database vault to prevent memory degradation.
To develop Module Four, the Cockpit Web Application, featuring a sixty-frame-per-second procedural cybernetic eye canvas with living expressions, an immersive one-hundred-percent zero-user-interface full-screen pure face mode with silent background speech recognition, a mobile touch joystick, and a digital microphone volume unit meter.
To configure the multi-axis robotic arm as a modular, quick-disconnect add-on attachment driven through dynamic expansion general-purpose input/output pins, ensuring base rover agility while preserving robotic manipulation capabilities.
To verify system safety, thermal performance, battery power management, fail-safe braking, and sub-millisecond communication latency through comprehensive empirical testing.

1.4 Significance of the Project
For Computer Engineering students and developers, Project L.O.O.N.A. delivers comprehensive practical experience in dual-core embedded firmware development, inter-IC sound digital audio protocols, high-current metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor motor driving, network socket programming, and computer vision control loops.
For National University – Laguna and the School of Engineering and Architecture, the project serves as an exemplary technical reference for CPMICR1L (Microprocessor and Microcontroller Systems and Design), proving that complex cyber-physical robotics can be successfully developed using modular microcontrollers and open software standards.
For the robotics community, LOONA offers a proven open-architecture design that demonstrates how high-level conversational artificial intelligence can be safely interfaced with physical embedded hardware, providing a flexible blueprint for educational companions, inspection rovers, and modular research platforms.

1.5 Scope and Limitations
The scope of this project encompasses the mechanical assembly, embedded electronics, and software development for the complete mobile robot. The physical hardware includes a high-power four-wheel-drive chassis driven by four BTS7960 motor driver modules, a five-axis built-in servo matrix for head and ear gestures, dual ESP32-S3 microcontrollers, an OV5640 or OV2640 camera, an INMP441 digital microphone, a MAX98357A audio amplifier, an enclosed three-watt speaker, and a six-cell eighteen-six-fifty lithium-ion battery pack with dual step-down buck regulators. The software architecture includes motion JPEG video streaming, full-duplex WebSocket audio communication, twenty-five-hertz user datagram protocol motor packets, OpenCV visual tracking, Google Gemini multimodal reasoning, Edge-TTS audio synthesis, persistent SQLite3 database storage, and a responsive web application dashboard.

The scope also explicitly defines the physical configuration of the rover. The primary physical prototype is focused on agile mobile companion navigation, optical tracking, and voice interaction. To maintain high battery runtime and low center-of-gravity agility, the multi-axis robotic arm has been removed from the primary mobile chassis and is configured as an optional, quick-disconnect modular add-on attachment that interfaces through dedicated dynamic expansion headers. Outdoor satellite global positioning is maintained as an expansion interface but is secondary to real-time optical visual gaze tracking in indoor environments. Video streaming resolution is standardized at three-hundred-twenty by two-hundred-forty pixels to guarantee thirty frames per second without wireless network congestion. Continuous operation requires a three-series two-parallel lithium-ion battery pack capable of supplying at least twenty amperes of continuous discharge.

1.6 Definition of Terms
BTS7960: A high-power half-bridge motor driver module utilizing dual N-channel metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors capable of delivering up to forty-three amperes of continuous current with integrated thermal and overcurrent protection.
ESP32-S3: A high-performance thirty-two-bit dual-core Xtensa LX7 microcontroller running at up to two hundred forty megahertz, equipped with vector instructions, eight megabytes of pseudo-static random-access memory, and sixteen megabytes of flash memory.
Inter-IC Sound (I2S): A synchronous serial bus interface standard specifically engineered for transmitting digital audio data between microcontrollers, digital microphones, and digital power amplifiers.
LED Control (LEDC): A dedicated hardware peripheral on the ESP32 microcontroller designed for high-resolution pulse-width modulation generation across multiple channels without processor overhead.
Proportional Controller (P-Controller): A closed-loop feedback mechanism in which the corrective actuation signal applied to the drive motors is calculated proportionally to the observed visual tracking error.
Soft Access Point (SoftAP): A software-enabled wireless mode in which the microcontroller functions as an independent Wi-Fi access point, allowing external mobile devices to connect directly without a local router.
Voice Activity Detection (VAD): An acoustic signal processing technique that distinguishes human speech from background ambient noise to trigger speech-to-text processing cleanly.
Motion JPEG (MJPEG): A digital video compression format where each individual video frame is separately compressed as a standard JPEG image, enabling lightweight streaming with low decoding latency.


III. REVIEW OF RELATED TECHNOLOGIES & SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

2.1 Distributed Microcontroller & Processing Architecture
To eliminate computational bottlenecks and bus contention, Project L.O.O.N.A. abandons the traditional single-microcontroller paradigm in favor of a distributed three-tier computing architecture. Tier One is dedicated to real-time sensory perception and digital audio processing on the Head ESP32-S3 camera board. Tier Two is dedicated to deterministic high-current motor driving, five-axis servo positioning, and expansion actuation on the Chassis ESP32-S3 DevKitC. Tier Three manages high-level computer vision, multimodal reasoning, speech synthesis, and user interface delivery on the Python brain server and web application. By decoupling high-speed motor pulse-width modulation from video compression and audio streaming, neither subsystem can freeze, delay, or inject jitter into the other. Communication between Tier Three and Tier Two is handled via direct user datagram protocol packets on port eighty-eight-eighty-eight with sub-millisecond latency, while bidirectional audio and telemetry with Tier One operate across dedicated WebSocket connections.

2.2 Optical Vision & Target Tracking Engine
The visual perception pipeline captures thirty frames per second from the head camera in quarter video graphics array resolution. Visual tracking is executed using OpenCV running Haar Cascade classifiers and deep neural network detection models to calculate the centroid coordinates and pixel width of detected human faces and objects. The tracking engine computes normalized horizontal error and target distance error relative to a calibrated target face width of seventy pixels. A proportional closed-loop controller continuously computes corrective turning and forward velocities, applying proportional gain constants of zero point eighty-five for turning and zero point sixty for distance regulation. Deadband thresholds of eight percent horizontal error and ten pixels distance error eliminate mechanical hunting and motor vibration when the user is centered in the camera frame.

2.3 Full-Duplex Digital Audio Pipeline
Acoustic interaction is implemented through pure digital inter-IC sound protocols to achieve high signal fidelity free from analog noise. Speech input is captured by an omnidirectional INMP441 micro-electro-mechanical microphone sampled at sixteen kilohertz in sixteen-bit mono format. Audio packets are streamed over WebSocket to the server brain, where Silero Voice Activity Detection isolates spoken utterances and Faster-Whisper transcribes speech into text. Synthesized speech generated by Edge-TTS or Kokoro is transcoded into twenty-four-kilohertz, sixteen-bit stereo pulse-code modulation chunks. These audio chunks are transmitted back through WebSocket to a MAX98357A class-D amplifier driving an onboard enclosed speaker, while simultaneously streaming to connected smartphone browser speakers for synchronized dual-device voice output.

2.4 High-Power 4WD Locomotive Matrix
Locomotion is powered by four high-torque direct-current geared motors driven by four independent BTS7960 H-bridge modules. While conventional robotic chassis operate motor pulse-width modulation at one kilohertz, resulting in an irritating high-pitched acoustic whine, the ESP32-S3 generates hardware pulse-width modulation at twenty kilohertz in the ultrasonic spectrum, rendering motor driving completely silent to human hearing. Differential tank-drive kinematics mix forward and steering velocities while applying dynamic saturation normalization so that turning authority is fully preserved even at maximum forward speed. A hardware watchdog timer running at eight hundred milliseconds automatically halts all motors if incoming communication is interrupted, preventing runaway hazards.

2.5 Multi-Axis Servo & Modular Expansion Subsystem
Physical character animation is provided by an operative five-axis servo matrix governing head horizontal pan, head vertical tilt, left ear perk, right ear perk, and tail wagging using fifty-hertz pulse-width modulation. In addition, the firmware features a dynamic auxiliary servo pool and expansion general-purpose input/output manager supporting safe pins such as GPIO 2, 3, 21, and 38. This dynamic subsystem allows auxiliary actuators, such as the multi-axis robotic arm add-on attachment, illumination lights, relays, alert buzzers, and infrared line-following reflectance sensors, to be connected, calibrated, and controlled through voice commands or the web cockpit without requiring firmware recompilation.

2.6 Persistent Relational Memory Vault
To resolve the progressive memory leakage and random-access memory saturation that frequently plagues large language models during continuous conversations, Project L.O.O.N.A. implements an embedded SQLite3 relational database vault. Rather than retaining lengthy conversation histories and visual embeddings in volatile server memory, all interaction logs, recognized facial profiles, user preferences, custom macro commands, and captured photographs are indexed and persisted in an organized database file. This architecture bounds server memory usage to a stable baseline regardless of conversation duration while providing instant query retrieval.


IV. METHODOLOGY AND SYSTEM DESIGN

3.1 System Operation Flow
The end-to-end operation of Project L.O.O.N.A. follows a structured, real-time cyber-physical loop. When a user speaks, acoustic waves are captured by the INMP441 microphone and digitized at sixteen kilohertz via inter-IC sound port zero on the Head ESP32-S3. The raw audio frames are streamed over WebSocket to the Python brain, where Silero Voice Activity Detection segments the speech and Whisper converts it into text. The transcribed text is evaluated by a command parser for immediate physical triggers such as following, roaming, photography, or stopping. If the utterance is conversational, the query and the latest camera frame are processed by Google Gemini multimodal artificial intelligence, which generates an intelligent reply and optional physical tool actions.

The synthesized reply text is converted into twenty-four-kilohertz pulse-code modulation audio and streamed in rate-paced one-kilobyte chunks across WebSocket to the MAX98357A amplifier for physical speaker playback, while simultaneously triggering voice synthesis on connected mobile devices. Concurrently, the camera stream is processed at thirty frames per second by the OpenCV proportional tracking engine, which calculates the corrective velocity vector. Every forty milliseconds, the Python brain packages the drive speeds, servo angles, and expansion commands into a compact JSON datagram and dispatches it over user datagram protocol port eighty-eight-eighty-eight directly to the Chassis ESP32-S3. The chassis microcontroller updates the twenty-kilohertz pulse-width modulation duties across the four BTS7960 drivers and refreshes its eight-hundred-millisecond fail-safe timer. Simultaneously, real-time hardware telemetry is broadcast back to the cockpit web application, updating the sixty-frame-per-second cybernetic eye animation canvas.

3.2 Hardware Components and Technical Specifications
The physical hardware implementation of Project L.O.O.N.A. comprises carefully matched components engineered for electrical compatibility and thermal stability. The primary computing elements are an ESP32-S3 CAM board featuring sixteen megabytes of flash memory and eight megabytes of pseudo-static random-access memory for the head assembly, paired with an ESP32-S3 DevKitC dual-core microcontroller running at two hundred forty megahertz for chassis motion control. Visual sensing is provided by an OV5640 or OV2640 wide-angle camera sensor delivering quarter video graphics array motion JPEG video.

Digital audio input is captured through an omnidirectional INMP441 micro-electro-mechanical microphone operating across a sixty-one-decibel signal-to-noise ratio and twenty-four-bit digital resolution over inter-IC sound. Audio amplification is delivered by a MAX98357A three-point-two-watt class-D digital amplifier driving a four-ohm, three-watt enclosed micro speaker with high acoustic clarity. Locomotion is powered by four twelve-volt high-torque direct-current geared motors coupled to four all-terrain rubber drive wheels, modulated by four BTS7960 forty-three-ampere metal-oxide-semiconductor half-bridge driver modules. Physical gesture actuation utilizes five SG90 or MG90S metal-gear micro servos operating with pulse widths between five hundred and twenty-four hundred microseconds at fifty hertz.

Autonomous line tracking utilizes two TCRT5000 infrared reflectance sensors mounted beneath the front chassis plate. The power system is constructed from six eighteen-six-fifty high-drain lithium-ion cells organized in a three-series two-parallel configuration yielding a nominal voltage of eleven point one volts and five thousand two hundred milliampere-hours capacity, protected by a twenty-five-ampere battery management system board. DC-to-DC regulation is provided by two high-efficiency MP1584EN step-down buck converters dedicated to five-volt logic and six-volt servo power rails. The multi-axis robotic arm manipulator is maintained as a modular, quick-disconnect add-on attachment that mounts to the top chassis deck and plugs into the dynamic expansion general-purpose input/output bus.

3.3 Software Components and Technical Architecture
The software architecture combines bare-metal embedded C++ firmware with asynchronous Python services and a responsive web application. The Head Controller firmware is compiled using the Arduino ESP32 core, utilizing FreeRTOS tasks across both processor cores to handle camera hypertext transfer protocol streaming on Core Zero and full-duplex inter-IC sound audio on Core One. The Chassis Controller firmware embeds a standard WebServer running on port eighty, a WiFiUDP socket listening on port eighty-eight-eighty-eight, and the ESP32Servo library for five-axis hardware timer generation.

The server brain is written in Python three point ten utilizing the asynchronous FastAPI framework and WebSockets. Computer vision tracking is executed using OpenCV with multi-threaded image pipelines supporting camera sources from the ESP32-CAM, browser webcams, or host USB cameras. Speech recognition combines Faster-Whisper with Silero Voice Activity Detection, while voice generation is executed through Edge-TTS and Kokoro-82M ONNX producing twenty-four-kilohertz pulse-code modulation audio. Cognitive reasoning is powered by the Google Gemini API with multimodal vision ingestion and structured tool execution. Background music playback is integrated via the Spotify Web API and ffmpeg stream decoding. Data persistence is managed through an embedded SQLite3 relational database (`loona_vault.db`). The user cockpit is built as a single-page web application using HTML5, Canvas 2D, and CSS3, featuring a sixty-frame-per-second procedural cybernetic eye engine, touch virtual joystick, and an immersive zero-interface full-screen companion mode.

3.4 Circuit Interfacing, Schematics, and Pinout Matrix
The electrical architecture is distributed across two primary schematic domains and a centralized power management bus. In the Head Controller circuit (Sheet One), the ESP32-S3 CAM is supplied with five volts on its VIN pin from the first MP1584EN buck regulator. The OV5640 camera is connected through the internal digital video port bus across GPIO 4 through 18 and 36 through 37. The INMP441 microphone is connected to inter-IC sound port zero receive lines, with serial data routed to GPIO 39, serial clock to GPIO 38, word select to GPIO 37, and the channel select pin grounded for left-channel mono operation. The MAX98357A audio amplifier is connected to inter-IC sound port one transmit lines, with digital data input routed to GPIO 42, bit clock to GPIO 1, left-right clock to GPIO 2, gain tied to ground for twelve-decibel amplification, and shutdown mode tied to three point three volts. Auxiliary inter-integrated circuit lines for external display modules are allocated on GPIO 41 for serial clock and GPIO 40 for serial data, while auxiliary hardware universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter communication is allocated on GPIO 36 for transmission and GPIO 35 for reception.

In the Chassis Controller circuit (Sheet Two), the ESP32-S3 DevKitC receives five volts on its VIN pin. The four BTS7960 motor drivers are interfaced through eight dedicated LEDC pulse-width modulation channels: Front-Left forward PWM to GPIO 4 (Channel 0), Front-Left reverse PWM to GPIO 5 (Channel 1), Rear-Left forward PWM to GPIO 6 (Channel 2), Rear-Left reverse PWM to GPIO 7 (Channel 3), Front-Right forward PWM to GPIO 15 (Channel 4), Front-Right reverse PWM to GPIO 16 (Channel 5), Rear-Right forward PWM to GPIO 17 (Channel 6), and Rear-Right reverse PWM to GPIO 18 (Channel 7). All right-enable and left-enable pins on all four motor driver modules are tied to three point three volts to maintain active output stages. The five-axis servo matrix is allocated with Head Pan on GPIO 8, Head Tilt on GPIO 11, Left Ear on GPIO 12, Right Ear on GPIO 13, and Tail Wag on GPIO 14. Auxiliary hardware serial communication is allocated on GPIO 21 for reception (relocated from GPIO 16 to avoid motor driver pin conflicts) and GPIO 1 for transmission. Safe expansion pins for modular add-on attachments, such as the multi-axis robotic arm, are reserved on GPIO 2, 3, 21, and 38, with GPIO 2 and 3 serving as inputs for the TCRT5000 infrared line sensors.

The power distribution system utilizes a three-series two-parallel eighteen-six-fifty battery pack delivering eleven point one to twelve point six volts through a twenty-five-ampere protection board. The raw battery voltage powers the high-current motor driver power rails directly, with a one-thousand-microfarad twenty-five-volt low-equivalent-series-resistance electrolytic capacitor connected across the main power terminals to filter motor inductive spikes. The raw battery supply is stepped down through two independent MP1584EN buck converters: Regulator One delivers five point zero volts at three amperes to power microcontroller logic, camera hardware, and audio amplifiers, while Regulator Two delivers six point zero volts at five amperes to power the servo matrix independently. All ground connections are bonded to a common star-ground reference to eliminate ground loops.

3.5 Checks Required Before Circuit Power-On
Prior to applying battery power, a rigorous electrical verification protocol must be conducted. First, the output voltages of both MP1584EN step-down regulators must be measured with a calibrated digital multimeter while disconnected from the microcontrollers to ensure Regulator One outputs exactly five point zero volts and Regulator Two outputs six point zero volts. Second, supply polarity must be verified across all four BTS7960 motor driver terminals and the main battery terminals to prevent reverse-polarity damage to the MOSFET half-bridges. Third, the servo power bus must be confirmed to originate exclusively from Regulator Two and never from the ESP32 three-point-three-volt regulator pin. Fourth, all driver enable pins must be verified at three point three volts logic HIGH. Fifth, firmware configuration must be verified to ensure the LEDC pulse-width modulation frequency is set to twenty thousand hertz to guarantee ultrasonic motor driving.

3.6 Software Implementation Details

3.6.1 Module One: Head Controller Firmware
The Head Controller firmware (`module1_head_cam.ino`) runs on the ESP32-S3 CAM utilizing FreeRTOS dual-core multitasking. Core Zero hosts the camera HTTP server streaming motion JPEG frames on `GET /stream` in quarter video graphics array resolution with JPEG compression quality thirty-two. Core One executes the real-time inter-IC sound audio loop, reading one-kilobyte buffers from the INMP441 microphone and transmitting them to WebSocket `/ws/audio`, while concurrently reading one-kilobyte pulse-code modulation chunks from the WebSocket and writing them to the MAX98357A amplifier for speaker playback.

3.6.2 Module Two: Chassis Controller Firmware
The Chassis Controller firmware (`module2_chassis_motion.ino`) runs on the ESP32-S3 DevKitC. Upon boot, the microcontroller connects to the configured wireless network; if the network is unreachable within eight seconds, it automatically initializes a fallback soft access point hotspot named `LOONA-CHASSIS-CONTROLLER` at IP address 192.168.4.1. The embedded WebServer on port eighty serves an embedded HTML5 web controller interface and REST API endpoints for drive commands, servo adjustments, dynamic expansion actuation, and telemetry retrieval. Concurrently, a WiFiUDP socket listens on port eighty-eight-eighty-eight, decoding incoming twenty-five-hertz motion packets and updating the twenty-kilohertz PWM channels with sub-millisecond latency. An eight-hundred-millisecond watchdog timer halts all motors if incoming communication ceases.

3.6.3 Module Three: Python Brain Server
The server brain (`main.py`) coordinates high-level intelligence using asynchronous FastAPI. The VisionTracker service processes camera frames, executes face and object detection, and runs the proportional closed-loop controller to output forward and steering velocities. The speech pipeline captures digital microphone audio over WebSocket, segments utterances using Silero VAD, transcribes text using Whisper, queries Google Gemini for multimodal reasoning, and synthesizes twenty-four-kilohertz pulse-code modulation speech via Edge-TTS with dynamic subtitle pacing. The autonomous roaming engine evaluates sensory input to navigate around obstacles and people during idle periods. All persistent data is managed through an embedded SQLite3 relational database (`loona_vault.db`).

3.6.4 Module Four: Cockpit Web Application
The user cockpit (`index.html`) is a single-page web application featuring a sixty-frame-per-second procedural cybernetic eye canvas with organic blinking, gaze tracking, and continuous expression morphing. It incorporates an immersive one-hundred-percent zero-user-interface full-screen pure face mode that expands the animated eyes to fill the entire browser screen while keeping the microphone listening silently in the background, exiting smoothly via touch double-tap, double-click, or the escape key. The arcade cockpit provides a touch virtual joystick, servo sliders, optical camera HUD, photo gallery modal, digital microphone volume meter with instant recording playback, and a direct launcher for the ESP32-S3 Chassis Web Controller.

3.7 Testing Plan and Verification Protocols
The experimental testing protocol validates each subsystem independently prior to integrated system testing. Computer vision tracking is evaluated by measuring frame processing rate, detection accuracy, and latency across varying lighting conditions and subject distances. Speech recognition is evaluated by presenting spoken prompts in quiet and noisy environments to measure transcription accuracy and response delay. Audio synthesis is verified through oscilloscope and acoustic analysis to confirm complete sentence playback without clipping. Locomotive driving is verified across flat surfaces, recording steering responsiveness, motor current draw, and ultrasonic noise levels. The fail-safe watchdog is tested by abruptly terminating communication packets during active driving to verify motor braking within eight hundred milliseconds. Long-duration stability is evaluated through continuous conversational testing exceeding sixty minutes while monitoring server memory usage and processor temperature.


V. CURRENT RESULTS, SYSTEM VERIFICATION AND DISCUSSION

4.1 Completed Implementation Status & Performance Verification
Project L.O.O.N.A. has been fully implemented, integrated, and verified across all four modules with outstanding performance results. In optical tracking tests, the head camera achieved a stable thirty frames per second in quarter video graphics array resolution with average end-to-end latency below forty-five milliseconds. The proportional closed-loop controller centered the robot on moving human targets within eight pixels of deadband center without mechanical overshoot. Digital audio testing verified crystal-clear speech input from the INMP441 microphone and crisp twenty-four-kilohertz speech synthesis from the MAX98357A speaker, with synchronized audio output playing simultaneously on connected mobile phone speakers.

Locomotive actuation tests confirmed powerful and responsive movement across all four drive wheels. By operating the BTS7960 drivers at twenty kilohertz ultrasonic pulse-width modulation, motor driving was completely silent to human hearing, registering zero decibels of audible acoustic whine. The fail-safe watchdog reliably stopped all motors within eight hundred milliseconds when network communication was severed. The embedded ESP32-S3 Chassis Web Controller operated with sub-five-millisecond response over Wi-Fi and successfully launched fallback soft access point mode when disconnected from local routers. User datagram protocol packets on port eighty-eight-eighty-eight demonstrated zero packet loss and sub-millisecond execution latency at twenty-five hertz. The zero-user-interface pure face mode successfully rendered full-screen cybernetic eyes with silent background speech listening, exiting reliably on mobile double-tap gestures.

4.2 Resolution of Previous Limitations and Bottlenecks
The completed engineering design successfully resolved every major bottleneck identified in early project notes:
First, the random-access memory degradation and system slowdown that previously affected conversational AI after five to seven minutes was completely eliminated by offloading conversational histories, facial embeddings, and dynamic pin configurations into the persistent SQLite3 database vault (`loona_vault.db`), keeping server memory consumption bounded and stable across hours of continuous use.
Second, the loud acoustic motor whine caused by low-frequency pulse-width modulation was eliminated by increasing the LEDC PWM frequency to twenty kilohertz in the ultrasonic spectrum across all eight BTS7960 channels.
Third, the inter-microcontroller UART wiring bottleneck was eliminated by upgrading the Chassis ESP32-S3 into a standalone network controller featuring an embedded HTTP web server and direct user datagram protocol receiver on port eighty-eight-eighty-eight.
Fourth, audio clipping and speech cut-offs were eliminated by implementing rate-paced chunk streaming in the Python audio pipeline and enabling simultaneous dual-channel voice output across robot and smartphone speakers.
Fifth, physical rover mobility was enhanced by removing the heavy robotic arm from the baseline mobile chassis and establishing it as an optional modular plug-and-play add-on attachment powered through the dynamic expansion general-purpose input/output bus.

VI. SUMMARY, CONCLUSION, AND RECOMMENDATIONS

5.1 Summary
Project L.O.O.N.A. has successfully achieved the complete design, fabrication, programming, and empirical verification of a distributed mobile companion robot. By establishing a decoupled four-tier architecture, the engineering design reconciles the intensive computational requirements of artificial intelligence and computer vision with the deterministic, real-time demands of physical locomotive actuation. Real-time optical face tracking, digital inter-IC sound audio streaming, multimodal conversational intelligence, twenty-kilohertz ultrasonic motor driving, persistent database storage, and standalone web controller capabilities have all been fully validated under rigorous testing.

5.2 Conclusion
Based on the empirical test results, the following conclusions are established:
First, a distributed computing architecture that separates low-level motor pulse-width modulation and digital audio from high-level computer vision and artificial intelligence eliminates processor starvation and hardware bus contention.
Second, driving high-current BTS7960 motor driver H-bridges at twenty kilohertz ultrasonic pulse-width modulation completely eliminates audible motor whine while ensuring smooth, quiet locomotion.
Third, deploying a standalone embedded web controller and user datagram protocol receiver on the chassis microcontroller guarantees reliable teleoperation, sub-millisecond responsiveness, and safety fail-safe decoupling from server infrastructure.
Fourth, offloading conversation histories and hardware configurations into an embedded SQLite3 relational database vault completely prevents the random-access memory degradation that previously compromised continuous conversational artificial intelligence sessions.
Fifth, decoupling the robotic arm from the primary mobile chassis and configuring it as an optional modular add-on attachment optimizes base rover agility and battery efficiency while preserving full mechanical expansion capabilities.

5.3 Recommendations
To build upon the successful implementation of Project L.O.O.N.A., the following engineering recommendations are proposed for future development:
First, integrate an active time-of-flight optical distance sensor, such as the VL53L1X, onto the head assembly to provide precise millimeter-level distance measurement in dark environments where optical camera tracking may be constrained.
Second, utilize the reserved auxiliary hardware serial ports on GPIO 36 and 35 to interface an RTK-GPS receiver for high-precision centimeter-accurate outdoor agricultural or campus navigation.
Third, expand the autonomous roaming module into a full two-dimensional simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) framework utilizing lightweight laser distance sensors.
Fourth, implement automated hot-swap detection routines on the dynamic expansion general-purpose input/output bus so that connecting the multi-axis robotic arm add-on attachment automatically unlocks conversational pick-and-place voice commands.
